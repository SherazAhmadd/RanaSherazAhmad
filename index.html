Research Interest/تحقیقی دلچسپی

Genetic Architecture and Molecular Mechanisms of Complex Diseases. Studying genetic variation, from single-nucleotide variants to large structural variants, to understand how it contributes to human complex diseases. I combine statistical genetics and computational genomics, including variant detection, association analysis, and functional interpretation of variants, and I am particularly interested in how risk variants identified at the population level translate into altered gene function and disease biology.

Multi-Omics Integration and Biomarker Discovery. Integrating genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, and clinical data to identify robust and interpretable biomarkers of disease risk and progression. I am interested in statistical and machine-learning frameworks that combine heterogeneous data layers while remaining explainable, and in how biomarkers discovered computationally can be validated and translated into mechanistic understanding of disease pathways and clinically actionable tools for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment response prediction.

Bioinformatics Tool Development and Genomic Foundation Models. As sequencing data grow in size and complexity, reliable bioinformatics tools and pipelines are essential for turning raw data into reproducible biological insight. I design and develop scalable, well-documented tools and workflows that automate genome analysis and make complex methods accessible to the wider research community. I also use genomic foundation models to learn sequence representations that capture functional and evolutionary signals for downstream genomic prediction tasks.

  • XtractPAV: An Automated Pipeline for Identifying Presence–Absence Variations across Multiple Genomes

    Rana Sheraz Ahmad, Muhammad Sadaqat, Muhammad Tahir-ul-Qamar*, Khalid S. Allemailem, Nagwan A. Alzunaidy | (Published in BMC Genomics) doi:10.1186/s12864-026-12909-2

    Code Repository (GitHub) Zenodo
    Presence-absence variations (PAVs) significantly influence phenotypic diversity across and within species by modulating functional modules associated with stress responsiveness, adaptation, and developmental processes. This modulation ultimately contributes to genetic diversity at both inter- and intra-species levels. However, existing tools available for detecting PAVs in assembled genomes possess limitations that hinder comprehensive analyses. These limitations include the absence of scalable workflows for multi-genome analysis, the imposition of stringent parameters regarding coverage, PAV length, and sequence identity, as well as the frequent necessity for manual integration. To address these challenges, we developed XtractPAV, an end-to-end pipeline that automates the extraction, annotation, and interactive visualization of PAVs across assembled genomes. XtractPAV was evaluated using assembled genomes from both eukaryotic and prokaryotic organisms, including Pyrus communis, Arabidopsis thaliana, Mus musculus, and Salmonella enterica, to assess its capability to detect presence-absence variations across diverse species. The performance of XtractPAV was benchmarked against other established pipelines, demonstrating an optimized workflow that allows comprehensive extraction and annotation of PAVs. To further validate PAVs, representative XtractPAV-identified PAVs in A. thaliana and P. communis were independently confirmed using WGS paired-end read mapping, demonstrating consistent query-specific insertion and deletion signatures at predicted loci in the reference genome. Notably, the pipeline successfully identified PAVs from the reference set and also revealed novel PAV regions overlapping with genes. Furthermore, the automated report generation feature of XtractPAV produces publication-ready summaries of PAV distributions alongside diverse interactive figures. The XtractPAV webpage is available at its project page.
  • BioVix: An Integrated Large Language Model Framework for Data Visualization, Graph Interpretation, and Literature-Aware Scientific Validation

    Muhammad Zain Butt, Rana Sheraz Ahmad, Emaan Fatima, Muhammad Tahir-ul-Qamar* | Scientific Reports (Under review) doi:10.64898/2026.02.03.703467

    BioRXiv Preprint Code Repository (GitHub) Deployment
    The application of Large Language Models (LLMs) for generating data visualizations through natural language interaction represents a promising advance in AI-assisted scientific analysis. However, existing LLM-based tools largely emphasize graph generation, while research workflows require not only visualization but also rigorous interpretation and validation against established scholarly evidence. Despite advances in visualization technologies, no single tool currently integrates literature references with visualization while also generating insights from graphical data. To address this gap, we present BioVix, a web-based LLM-driven framework that integrates interactive data visualization, natural-language querying, and automated retrieval of relevant academic literature. BioVix enables users to upload datasets, generate complex visualizations, interpret graphical patterns, and contextualize findings through literature references within a unified workflow. The system employs a multi-model architecture combining DeepSeek V3.1 for code and logic generation, Qwen2.5-VL-32B-Instruct for multimodal interpretation, and GPT-OSS-20B for conversational reasoning, coordinated through structured prompt engineering. BioVix was evaluated across diverse biological domains, including proteomic expression profiling, epigenomic peak annotation, and clinical diabetes data, demonstrating its flexibility in handling heterogeneous datasets and supporting exploratory, literature-aware analysis. While BioVix substantially streamlines exploratory research workflows, its LLM-generated outputs are intended to support, not replace, expert judgment, and users should independently verify results before scientific reporting.
  • Genome-Wide Identification, Evolutionary Dynamics, and Functional Insights of LTR Retrotransposons in Three Mango (Mangifera indica) Cultivars

    Muhammad Sadaqat, Rana Sheraz Ahmad, Muhammad Tahir-ul-Qamar* | BMC Plant Biology (Under review)

    Code Repository (GitHub) Zenodo
    Long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTR-RTs) are a major class of transposable elements in plant genomes, influencing genome size, structure, and evolution. To gain insights into their diversity and evolutionary significance in mango, we analyzed the genomes of three cultivars, including Alphonso, Hong Xiang Ya, and Tommy Atkins, using the PlantLTR-Scan pipeline developed in this study, which integrates LTR-RTs detection, classification, insertion time estimation, phylogenetic analysis, and functional annotations of gene-inserted LTR-RTs. Through this pipeline, a total of 970, 889, and 633 intact LTR-RTs were identified in the three cultivars, respectively. Superfamily-level classification revealed a higher abundance of Copia elements compared to Gypsy, although Gypsy elements were significantly larger. Clade-level analysis showed Ivana and Ale as the dominant Copia families, while Reina and Tekay predominated among Gypsy elements, with notable cultivar-specific variation. Insertion time estimation indicated that the major amplification burst occurred ~ 0.5 Mya, contributing to recent genome expansion. Functional analysis of gene-associated LTR-RTs demonstrated preferential accumulation in intronic regions, with enrichment of functions such as phosphorylation, DNA binding, ATP binding, and microtubule-based movement. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that Ivana (Copia) and Reina (Gypsy) families are transcriptionally active, suggesting their ongoing contribution to genome plasticity. Comparative analyses with other plant species further highlighted both conserved patterns, such as the larger size of Gypsy elements, and lineage-specific amplification trajectories in mango. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LTR-RTs have shaped genome structure, contributed to functional diversity, and remain important drivers of genome evolution in mango cultivars.

    Abbas Khan, Adel Ibrahim A Alalawy, Zainab Rehman, M.A. Sawali, Rana Sheraz Ahmad, Fahad M Almutairi, Abdul Wadood, Abdelali Agouni* | RSC Advances (Submitted).

PLINK format, in particular the bed/fam/bim bundle for genotype data, is one of the most popular formats to store genotype information, among a couple of others such as VCF (and derivatives from it) and bgen.

PLINK is like a Swiss army knife for statistical genetics: it does lots of extremely useful routine data analysis, particularly data cleaning and exploratory analysis. That's why it is easy to start an analysis in PLINK format in order to use features offered in PLINK to understand the basics of data.

I think a general approach in learning (maybe anything) is to unbox the black box through looking at some simple examples. My suggestion is, try to install the snpStats package in R and use this function:

https://www.rdocumentation.org/packages/snpStats/versions/1.22.0/topics/read.plink

on a small data-set here:

https://github.com/statgenetics/orientation/tree/main/data/LMM_MWE

the genotype21_22 data bundle. See in R what you get!

In essence, the fam file contains sample information and the bim file contains variant information, both in plain text format that you can open with a text editor (in case you did not realize!). They only have a few columns, so they should be easy to understand if you look at the PLINK documentation. The bed file is a sample-by-variant matrix of genotypes, but saved in binary format, which may look like a black box. That's why I suggested loading it with tools such as the aforementioned R package to look into it.

I would also like to point out that PLINK has a few types of file bundles. Most popular are bed/bim/fam (best compression, with bed being a binary file) and ped/map (pure text format, stemmed from the traditional LINKAGE analysis software input). These bundles contain the same information and can be converted between each other using PLINK.

Genotype data from different arrays, labs or imputation runs rarely line up out of the box. The same SNP can be on a different genome build, reported on the opposite strand, or have its alleles swapped. Merging without checking these produces silent errors that later look like real association signals or population structure. These are the checks I run, in order, before merging two datasets A and B.

1. Same genome build

Compare the positions of a few well-known SNPs (for example from dbSNP) in both datasets. If they differ, lift one dataset over to the other build (GRCh37 ↔ GRCh38) with a tool such as CrossMap or bcftools +liftover before anything else.

2. Normalise against the reference genome

Split multi-allelic sites, left-align indels, and make the REF allele match the reference genome, flipping strand where needed and dropping sites that cannot be matched:

bcftools norm -m -any -f ref.fa A.vcf.gz -Oz -o A.norm.vcf.gz
bcftools +fixref A.norm.vcf.gz -Oz -o A.fixref.vcf.gz -- -f ref.fa -m flip -d

3. Give every variant the same ID

rsIDs differ between dbSNP versions, so use position and alleles as the ID in both datasets:

bcftools annotate --set-id '%CHROM:%POS:%REF:%FIRST_ALT' A.fixref.vcf.gz -Oz -o A.ids.vcf.gz

4. Remove strand-ambiguous SNPs

A/T and C/G SNPs look the same on both strands, so a strand flip cannot be detected from the alleles alone. Unless you can resolve them by allele frequency, drop them:

AMBIGUOUS='(REF="A" && ALT="T") || (REF="T" && ALT="A")'
AMBIGUOUS="$AMBIGUOUS"' || (REF="C" && ALT="G") || (REF="G" && ALT="C")'
bcftools view -e "$AMBIGUOUS" A.ids.vcf.gz -Oz -o A.qc.vcf.gz

5. Keep the shared variants and merge in PLINK

plink --vcf A.qc.vcf.gz --make-bed --out A
plink --vcf B.qc.vcf.gz --make-bed --out B
cut -f2 A.bim | sort > A.snps; cut -f2 B.bim | sort > B.snps
comm -12 A.snps B.snps > shared.snps
plink --bfile A --extract shared.snps --make-bed --out A.shared
plink --bfile B --extract shared.snps --make-bed --out B.shared
plink --bfile A.shared --bmerge B.shared --make-bed --out merged

If the merge fails, PLINK writes the offending variants to merged-merge.missnp. Flip them in one dataset and try again; whatever still fails is excluded:

plink --bfile B.shared --flip merged-merge.missnp --make-bed --out B.flip
plink --bfile A.shared --bmerge B.flip --make-bed --out merged

6. Check allele frequencies

For samples of similar ancestry, a variant whose frequency is very different between the two sources (as a rule of thumb, more than 0.2) is more likely a strand or allele error than biology. Compare plink --freq output from both datasets and remove such variants.

7. Quality control after merging

plink --bfile merged --geno 0.05 --mind 0.05 --maf 0.01 --hwe 1e-6 --make-bed --out merged.qc
plink --bfile merged.qc --pca 10 --out merged.qc

Plot the first principal components and colour the samples by source. If the datasets separate by source rather than by ancestry, there is a batch effect left to fix.

A reference list of the software commonly used in the lab, grouped by analysis step, from raw sequencing reads to association, fine-mapping and post-GWAS analysis. Each name links to the tool's official page.

Next-generation sequencing

  • FastQC: quality control for high-throughput sequence data.
  • MultiQC: aggregates QC reports from many samples and tools into one report.
  • fastp: fast adapter trimming, filtering and QC of FASTQ files.
  • BWA: alignment and mapping of short reads against a reference genome.
  • bwa-mem2: a faster, drop-in replacement for BWA-MEM.
  • minimap2: alignment of long reads and genome assemblies.
  • samtools: manipulation of sequence alignments in SAM/BAM/CRAM format.
  • Picard: manipulating high-throughput sequencing data and formats.
  • GATK: variant discovery from sequence alignments in BAM format.
  • DeepVariant: deep-learning variant caller for short and long reads.
  • VCFtools: manipulation and summary statistics of VCF files.
  • bcftools: a faster replacement for VCFtools that also calls variants.
  • tabix: indexing and querying of tab-delimited genomic position files.
  • IGV: interactive visualization of sequence alignments and variants.

Variant annotation

  • ANNOVAR: functional annotation of genetic variants.
  • wANNOVAR: a web server for ANNOVAR.
  • SnpEff: variant annotation and effect prediction.
  • Ensembl VEP: Variant Effect Predictor, annotation against Ensembl transcripts.
  • CADD: deleteriousness scores for SNVs and indels.
  • Biosig tools: structure-based prediction of the effects of missense variants.

Genotype management and GWAS

  • PLINK 1.9 and PLINK 2: the standard toolkit for genotype data management, QC and GWAS.
  • bedtools and BEDOPS: manipulation of genomic intervals.

Linkage analysis

  • LINKAGE: single-point parametric linkage analysis.
  • MERLIN: multipoint linkage analysis of dense marker maps.
  • GENEHUNTER: multipoint linkage analysis.

Genotype phasing and imputation

  • IMPUTE2: genotype imputation and haplotype phasing.
  • Eagle: fast haplotype phasing, with or without a reference panel.
  • SHAPEIT5: haplotype phasing for large cohorts and sequencing data.
  • Beagle: phasing and imputation in one tool.
  • MaCH: haplotype-based genotype imputation.
  • Minimac3 and Minimac4: fast imputation into pre-phased data.
  • Michigan Imputation Server: a web server for fast and efficient phasing and imputation.
  • TOPMed Imputation Server: imputation with the TOPMed reference panel.

R libraries

  • Bioconductor: a bioinformatics toolkit written in R.
  • snpStats: reading PLINK files and SNP association analysis in R.
  • CRAN Task View: Bayesian Inference: a curated list of Bayesian inference packages.

Fine-mapping

  • susieR: Sum of Single Effects regression for fine-mapping.
  • FINEMAP: shotgun stochastic search for causal variants from summary statistics.
  • PolyFun: functionally informed fine-mapping with SuSiE or FINEMAP.

QTL mapping

  • Matrix eQTL: ultra-fast eQTL analysis by matrix operations.
  • FastQTL: fast cis-QTL mapping with permutation testing.
  • tensorQTL: GPU-accelerated QTL mapping.
  • QTLtools: a complete toolkit for molecular QTL discovery.

LD score regression and polygenic scores

  • LDSC: heritability and genetic correlation from GWAS summary statistics.
  • LDpred2: polygenic scores from summary statistics (the maintained successor of LDpred).
  • PRS-CS: polygenic prediction with continuous shrinkage priors.

Transcriptome-wide association (TWAS)

  • FUSION: a wrapper for several TWAS expression prediction methods.
  • S-PrediXcan: gene-level association from GWAS summary statistics.

Linear mixed models

  • BOLT-LMM: mixed-model association for large cohorts.
  • fastGWA: resource-efficient mixed-model GWAS in GCTA.
  • SAIGE: mixed models for binary traits with case-control imbalance.
  • REGENIE: whole-genome regression for quantitative and binary traits.
  • GEMMA: exact mixed-model association tests.

Visualization

  • BioRender: professional figures for the biological sciences.
  • LocusZoom: regional association plots.
  • qqman: Manhattan and Q-Q plots for GWAS results in R.
  • ggplot2: the grammar-of-graphics plotting system in R.

Further resources

  • The Rockefeller List: a comprehensive, searchable catalogue of genetic analysis programs.
  • CRAN Task View: Bayesian Inference: R packages for Bayesian analysis.

Structural variants (SVs) are changes in the structure of a genome rather than in single bases. By convention they are 50 bp or larger, and together they affect more of the genome than SNPs do. The main types are:

  • Deletions and insertions (indels): sequence lost or gained at one position. Indels shorter than 50 bp are usually called alongside SNPs; longer ones are SVs.
  • Copy number variants (CNVs): a region present in more or fewer copies than in the reference, through deletion or duplication (tandem or dispersed).
  • Inversions: a segment flipped in orientation, with no change in copy number.
  • Translocations: a segment moved to another position or chromosome.

Three ways to detect SVs

  • Short reads (Illumina): SVs are inferred indirectly, from read pairs that map too far apart or in the wrong orientation, from reads split across a breakpoint, and from changes in coverage (the main signal for CNVs). Tools include Manta, Delly and CNVnator.
  • Long reads (Oxford Nanopore, PacBio): single reads span most SVs, so they are seen directly. Tools include Sniffles2 and cuteSV.
  • Genome assemblies: when two chromosome-level assemblies exist, aligning one to the other shows every rearrangement between them, including large inversions and translocations that reads struggle to resolve.

Here I use the assembly approach with SyRI (Synteny and Rearrangement Identifier; Goel et al., Genome Biology 2019). From a whole-genome alignment it separates syntenic regions from inversions, translocations and duplications, and reports the smaller differences nested within them: SNPs, indels and highly diverged regions. The steps below compare a query assembly (qry.fa) with a reference assembly (ref.fa) on Linux.

conda create -n syri -c conda-forge -c bioconda syri plotsr minimap2 samtools
conda activate syri

1. Prepare the assemblies

SyRI expects both assemblies to be chromosome-level, with homologous chromosomes sharing the same name and the same orientation. Keep only the chromosomes, drop unplaced scaffolds and check that the names match:

samtools faidx ref.fa
samtools faidx qry.fa
cut -f1,2 ref.fa.fai
cut -f1,2 qry.fa.fai

If a query chromosome is reverse-complemented relative to the reference, flip it before aligning; otherwise the whole chromosome is reported as one large inversion.

2. Whole-genome alignment

Align the query to the reference with minimap2 in assembly mode. --eqx writes =/X CIGAR operations, which SyRI needs to distinguish matches from mismatches. Use asm5 for closely related genomes (under ~1% divergence) and asm10 or asm20 for more divergent ones:

minimap2 -ax asm5 --eqx -t 16 ref.fa qry.fa | samtools sort -@ 8 -o ref_qry.bam
samtools index ref_qry.bam

3. Identify synteny and rearrangements

syri -c ref_qry.bam -r ref.fa -q qry.fa -F B --prefix ref_qry. --nc 8

This writes ref_qry.syri.out (a tab-separated table of every region) and ref_qry.syri.vcf (the same calls as a VCF). Column 11 of the table holds the annotation: SYN syntenic, INV inversion, TRANS/INVTR translocation, DUP/INVDP duplication, plus nested SNP, INS, DEL and HDR (highly diverged region).

4. Summarise the calls

# how many regions of each type
cut -f11 ref_qry.syri.out | sort | uniq -c | sort -k1,1nr

# inversions of at least 10 kb, with their reference and query coordinates
awk -F'\t' '$11 == "INV" && $3 - $2 >= 10000 {print $1, $2, $3, $6, $7, $8}' \
  OFS='\t' ref_qry.syri.out

# insertions and deletions of at least 50 bp
awk -F'\t' '($11 == "INS" || $11 == "DEL") && (length($4) >= 50 || length($5) >= 50)' \
  ref_qry.syri.out > ref_qry.indels50.tsv

5. Visualise with plotsr

plotsr, from the same group, draws synteny and rearrangements between the genomes. It reads a small table listing the assemblies in order:

printf '#file\tname\nref.fa\tReference\nqry.fa\tQuery\n' > genomes.txt
plotsr --sr ref_qry.syri.out --genomes genomes.txt -o ref_qry.plotsr.png

Grey bands are syntenic regions; inversions, translocations and duplications are drawn in their own colours, so large rearrangements stand out at a glance. For more than two genomes, run SyRI on each consecutive pair and pass all the .syri.out files to plotsr with repeated --sr options, listing the genomes in the same order.

Students often ask me how to get started with research in computational biology. Most of them have taken a few bioinformatics courses, used BLAST or a web server, and now want to do something real but do not know where to begin. This post is the advice I would give my younger self during my B.S. at GCUF.

1. Start with a biological question, not a tool

Good computational biology research begins with a question: which genes are differentially expressed under drought stress, which variants are associated with a disease, how genome structure differs between two cultivars. Tools and pipelines are only the means to answer it. Pick an area of biology that genuinely interests you and read a few recent reviews in it before you open a terminal.

2. Build the core skills

  • Linux command line: navigating files, grep, awk, sed, pipes and shell scripts. Almost every bioinformatics tool runs here.
  • One programming language, properly: Python or R. Learn data handling (pandas or the tidyverse) and plotting before anything fancy.
  • Statistics: distributions, hypothesis testing, multiple testing correction and regression. Most mistakes in genomics papers are statistical, not computational.
  • Version control: put every project on Git and GitHub from day one.
  • Working on a server or HPC: SSH, job schedulers such as SLURM, and environment managers such as conda.

3. Learn by reproducing a published analysis

The fastest way to learn is to pick a paper in your area that uses public data, download the raw data from NCBI GEO, SRA or ENA, and try to reproduce one of its main figures. You will run into missing parameters, broken links and version conflicts, and solving those problems is exactly what research feels like. Write down every command you run.

4. Find a mentor and a lab

Research is much easier with guidance. Look for faculty whose recent papers you actually understand and find interesting. When you email them, keep it short: who you are, which of their papers you read, what you found interesting, the skills you already have, and a link to your GitHub. A specific, honest email gets a reply far more often than a generic one sent to fifty people.

5. Start small and finish

Your first project does not need to be groundbreaking. A small, well-defined analysis that you complete, document and write up teaches more than an ambitious project that never ends. Keep a lab notebook (a Markdown file is enough), organize your folders by raw data, scripts and results, and make your work reproducible so that someone else could rerun it.

6. Read papers regularly

Set aside time every week to read. For each paper, ask what question it answers, what data and methods it uses, and what its limitations are. Read the methods section carefully, since that is where you learn which tools people actually use and why.

7. Share your work

Put your code on GitHub with a clear README, present at student symposia and conferences, and write up your results, even as a preprint. Writing forces you to understand your own analysis, and visible work is what opens doors to the next position.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Running tools with default parameters without understanding what they do.
  • Skipping quality control of the input data.
  • Trying to learn every tool instead of mastering a few.
  • Working alone for months without showing your results to anyone.

Useful resources

  • Rosalind: bioinformatics programming problems.
  • Software Carpentry: lessons on the shell, Git, Python and R.
  • EMBL-EBI Training: free courses on databases and analysis methods.
  • Biostars: a Q&A forum for bioinformatics.
  • My Bioinformatics Tutorials: hands-on walkthroughs of the workflows I use.

Everyone starts somewhere. Be curious, be consistent, and do not be afraid to ask questions.
